Risk-Limiting Audit Lab

A risk-limiting audit checks a reported election outcome by hand-inspecting a random sample of paper ballots. It keeps sampling until the evidence that the reported winner really won is strong enough, or it escalates to a full hand count. Change the settings below and watch how the margin, the number of ballots and the risk limit drive the work an audit takes.

How a ballot-polling audit works

This lab uses BRAVO, the standard ballot-polling method (Lindeman, Stark & Yates, 2012), for a two-candidate contest.

  1. Start with the reported results: Candidate A won with share s of the votes.
  2. Draw a paper ballot at random and read it by hand. A vote for A multiplies the test statistic T by 2s; a vote for B multiplies it by 2(1−s).
  3. If T reaches 1/α (α is the risk limit), stop: the outcome is confirmed.
  4. If the sample gets too large first, escalate to a full hand count, which finds the right winner.

The risk limit α is the largest chance that the audit confirms a wrong outcome. With α = 5%, a wrong outcome gets past the audit at most 5% of the time.

Exercises

Use the buttons to load each setup, then run the audits. Write down what you see before opening the discussion note.

  1. The risk limit. With a 10-point margin and 100,000 ballots, run 1,000 audits at α = 20%, 10%, 5% and 1%. How does the median sample size change? Why does a stricter risk limit cost more ballots?
    Discussion

    The audit stops when T reaches 1/α, so a stricter limit raises the bar from 5 to 100. Because each ballot moves T by a small multiplicative step, the sample grows with log(1/α): going from 10% to 1% roughly doubles the work, not tenfold.

  2. Closeness. Keep α = 5%. Compare margins of 20, 10, 5 and 2 points. What happens to the sample each time the margin is cut in half?
    Discussion

    Halving the margin roughly quadruples the expected sample. Each ballot carries less evidence when the race is close, so very close races need very large samples, and many escalate to a full hand count. That is one reason close races often go to recounts.

  3. Number of ballots. With a 5-point margin and α = 5%, compare 10,000 ballots with 10,000,000. Does the number of ballots sampled change much? What does change?
    Discussion

    The sample needed is about the same (a few thousand ballots) whether 10,000 or 10 million were cast, just as a poll of 1,000 people works for a city or a country. What changes is the share: a few thousand ballots is a big slice of a small county and a tiny slice of a large state. Audits are cheap for big contests and comparatively expensive for small ones.

  4. A wrong outcome. Make the report wrong: officials announced A won by 4 points, but B actually won by 1. Run 1,000 audits at α = 10%, then at α = 5%. How often does the audit confirm the wrong winner?
    Discussion

    The audit should wrongly confirm at most α of the time, and usually far less. Almost every audit escalates to a full hand count, which corrects the outcome. This is the guarantee that gives the method its name: the risk of certifying a wrong winner is limited to α.

  5. Right winner, smaller margin. The report says A won by 10 points, but the paper shows A won by only 4 (for example, a tabulation error). The outcome is still right. How does the audit behave compared with exercise 2?
    Discussion

    The audit still confirms the right winner, but it needs more ballots than the reported margin would suggest, and some audits escalate. Ballot-polling audits are tuned to the reported margin; when the paper disagrees with the report, the evidence piles up more slowly.

  6. Very close. Set a 0.5-point margin with 1,000,000 ballots and α = 5%. Look at the planning chart and run a few single audits. What would you recommend to an election official?
    Discussion

    The expected sample is on the order of a hundred thousand ballots, and many audits escalate anyway. For margins this small, a full hand count (a recount) may be simpler. Ballot-comparison audits, which check each sampled paper ballot against its cast vote record, need far fewer ballots and are the usual answer for close contests.

Method: BRAVO ballot-polling audit (Lindeman, Stark & Yates, “BRAVO: Ballot-polling Risk-limiting Audits to Verify Outcomes,” EVT/WOTE 2012), two candidates, ballots drawn without replacement. Expected sample sizes on the planning chart use the standard approximation ln(1/α) ÷ [s·ln(2s) + (1−s)·ln(2(1−s))].
